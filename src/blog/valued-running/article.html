!markdown # Running by Values As I write this, it's the pre-season for the 2026/27 cross country season. When I was younger, I set goals for what I wanted to achieve, but more recently, I have been putting values before goals. This has positively changed my relationship with running, and my goals look very different. Previously, I may have set position targets for races, but now I aim to be closer to the front at the end than I was halfway through (a negative position split). This aligns much more with my value of being controlled. Over the summer, values helped me a lot when injury in the winter limited my fitness. I neglected my values at the start, focusing instead on my performance from the year before. I remember doing a session of 5x4 mins and trying to run the whole session at 72 second laps. Last year, I would have found this comfortable. This time, I could only manage three reps at that pace before fading to 75 second laps for the final two reps. I reflected on this, and realised that I'd been focusing on the outcome instead of the process. One of my values is doing what I can do each day, so two weeks later, when we did 4 min reps again, I aimed at going slower and doing 73 seconds per lap. This was a more realistic pace, and I was able to hold the pace for 6 reps. Not only did I get a bigger physical benefit the second time, I was buzzing afterwards, delighted that I had managed to align with my values. Goals define what you want to achieve, values describe how you want to behave. Having just goals limits each event to a success/failure that may be out of your control. You always have the opportunity to act according to your values. If your goals are to run fast in the summer, it may be difficult to stay motivated over many months, and it may feel like running 6 miles instead of 8 one day will have no effect on your future performance. But with a value of focusing on the small details, those extra 2 miles will reward you with a lot of satisfaction. You can also achieve your values by not running. For example, if you have a value to listen to your body when you're carrying a niggle or feeling ill, you may choose to rest. You may not always be able to follow your values, but two of my most important values are forgiveness and reflection. I often make mistakes and fall into old habits, but that's okay - I have another chance next time. If you’re curious about trying this yourself, I’ve found these two exercises especially helpful for deciding on my values. The first is called "Award Ceremony": you imagine that you win an award, and people give mini speeches about you. What things do they say? The things you want people to say about you are often a reflection of your values. The second exercise is to think about qualities you admire in people around you. Those qualities will again help you reveal your values. Doing these exercises has allowed me to see a greater team aspect in running, even though it’s predominantly an individual sport. Nevertheless, deciding on my values isn’t easy. I’ve improved a lot with practice, but it still takes me a decent amount of time and effort. Values are truly your own - even if other people suggest them, you decide what your values are. Once you have them, it's a personal commitment to follow them. At points, you can ask yourself which values you are following or not, and which you want to focus more on in the near future. When the values have set the direction, you may want goals to act as steps, but this isn't necessary. Each goal should align with your values and can help you remain faithful to them. Values may not work for everyone. However, there’s a large body of psychological research that supports their effectiveness. For me, they have given me a lot more fulfilment and enjoyment from running. I felt that I deeply wanted to share that perspective with other people. It stays true to my value of supporting others.
