!markdown # Alpine ARC This summer cemented the observation that getting anything nontrivial right the first time is impossible (which is why you're reading the twentieth or so revision of this blog post). [Last summer](/blog/summer-project/) was fantastic, but I knew that this summer could be even more powerful. There were aspects to improve, such as pair programming more effectively and collaborating more equally. And fresh challenges, such as the concrete metric and external expectations. This was the first time I had lived with Claire, and this intense collaboration provided a new set of life experiences. Over the summer, I spent three months in France with my girlfriend, Claire. We dedicated our time to working on the ARC prize, which involves writing a program to find patterns for a small set of example grid problems. ![ARC example problem](/images/alpine-arc/ARC-puzzle.png "Example ARC Puzzle") It's easy for humans, but very hard for computers. Have a look at some more of the problems here: [https://arcprize.org/play](https://arcprize.org/play). In the last year, I learned lots and developed new tools. I practised exercises from Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT) and Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), particularly acceptance and willingness, which I found the most transformative. This enhanced my ability to iterate on my problem-solving processes and develop new routines and practices. These supplemented the wisdom I had gained from the previous summer. After a final year of battling through the degree, the environment became much more favourable. Our location on the edge of the Alps reignited my love for mountains and began to fuel Claire's. Otherwise, minimal distractions gave us time and space for prolonged focus on the ARC problems. One of the most important changes after university was the absence of judgement from authority. Fresh ideas often sound foolish at first - Airbnb, for example, struggled to convince investors that letting strangers stay in your home could become a profitable business. Yet my entire life up to that point had been shaped by appealing to authority, producing the expected result rather than surprising ones. On ARC, that was futile: conventional ideas didn't work. We still faced obstacles. The only way to obtain a whiteboard involved a 7 km run, followed by another 7 km run carrying the whiteboard. Grenoble was a pleasant temperature when we arrived, but a week later, there was an unbearable heatwave. Even last year's heatwave had not prepared me for this. And Claire had never experienced anything close to it. The direct consequences made it difficult to sleep and work. But this led to a necessary afternoon nap, slashing our productivity and feeding a cycle that hindered meaningful progress. The stress of being unable to work only made it harder to do so. ARC was already a formidable problem, and this caused Claire and me a new spectrum of difficult emotions. Weeks passed, and we produced seemingly nothing. Our ideas seemed uninteresting and led nowhere. We didn't know what to try or when to move on to different ideas. The few approaches we saw appealed to us differently, and we struggled to balance pursuing what each of us found interesting. Even though I knew these setbacks were part of the process, the persistent lack of progress was demoralising. However, I had a realisation that made things a lot better. For the first time in my life, scoring zero was acceptable. It was liberating. We had chosen to ignore what existed and focus on our own ideas. Everyone else was building ladders to the moon, while we were jumping up and down like idiots, suggesting that the ladders might not work. Eventually, we had an idea that we liked. It was just a tiny piece of the puzzle. It was a way to describe the solution to each problem more clearly in a way that was detached from an algorithm or code implementation. This seemingly innocuous thought turned into a breakthrough. Suddenly, we started getting somewhere. It didn't even constitute a full solution, but it gave us a foothold to begin climbing. Our outlook changed drastically after this. Claire started sleeping better (partially aided by the pandas). We spent more time whiteboarding and were more satisfied with the ideas. We pushed far with this idea, and the version we had at the end no longer resembled the original. We realised that our failures early on had been instrumental in guiding our discoveries and shaping our insights. It led to other satisfying breakthroughs, for example, we had a modular setup and were able to replace one component with a faster, simpler and better-performing algorithm. We evaluated our progress using the previous year's dataset[^arc-dataset]. We verified that each new feature led to an improvement, even though our overall score on the test set remained at zero. We transformed from solving just one problem initially to solving a significant percentage of the original dataset. Like last summer, I developed immensely. Our process improved, and so did the quality of the artefacts. For example, we started rewriting our pseudocode several times, alternating editing each revision. The end version was always significantly easier to read, modify and test than the first. We made leaps forward in our emotional skills, such as the way we responded to feedback while pairing. Working with Claire was once again a thrilling experience. I became much more open to her insights, and found the most valuable ideas came from both of us, not really knowing or caring who suggested the original seed. The shared adventure deepened our relationship, and we grew both individually and together. The summer sparked a lot of new project ideas. I wanted to do them justice, but ARC was the main focus. Now, I'm giving myself time to work on them and develop them fully, and I'll go back to ARC at some point. I feel like we've only scratched the surface. [^arc-dataset]: ARC consists of two datasets. The first one had previously run as a competition and the second was designed afterwards to be much harder.
